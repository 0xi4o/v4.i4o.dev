**The win for today:** the DocuDex design doc — now with POC results, two
ADRs and two diagrams — sent for review with a five-line header that says
what feedback you want, who decides, and when it closes; then, a week later,
a written review outcome. About thirty minutes now and thirty when the
comments are in. This is the lesson the tech-lead role is actually testing.

## What to do (read this first)

1. Put the five-line header from the [review request reference](../reference/review-request)
   at the top of the DocuDex doc. Fill every line.
2. Choose reviewers. For a solo project with no senior peers to hand, the
   review is public: r/golang, the Go Forum (forum.golangbridge.org) and
   Gophers Slack `#tools`, all pointing at one public URL for the doc, ADRs,
   POC and diagrams so comments land in one place. No approver — say so in
   the header; you are the decider and everyone replying is input. One post
   text, written as the "wanted" line, one close date for all three.
3. When comments arrive, sort every one into challenge / gap / evidence /
   preference / nit before replying to any. Answer challenges and gaps in the
   doc, then point to the doc from the thread.
4. On the close date, write the **Review outcome** section and send one
   broadcast message. Change the status.

<KeyIdea>
	A review produces useful comments in proportion to how precisely you asked for them. "Any
	feedback welcome" gets typos and preferences. "Challenge the storage decision in §3; I am not
	looking for naming feedback" gets a reviewer's one real objection — which is the thing you
	could not see yourself.
</KeyIdea>

## 1. What the process is for (4 min)

Ubl's [Design Docs at Google](https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/)
describes review as comment threads on the doc, and gives the reason it
exists: to bring the organisation's experience to bear "early, before
expensive changes become necessary". The review is not a gate; it is the
cheapest moment to be wrong.

Sourcegraph's [handbook](https://github.com/sourcegraph/handbook/blob/main/content/company-info-and-process/communication/rfcs/index.md)
adds the two things that make it work asynchronously: a status label that
tells reviewers what kind of reading to do ("Reviewing problem" means
"confirm the problem definition is correct"; "Reviewing solution" means the
problem is agreed), and named roles — "the decider, input providers,
approvers" — plus tags for teams that are affected. HashiCorp's
[writing practices](https://www.hashicorp.com/en/how-hashicorp-works/articles/writing-practices-and-culture)
add the order: the author's own team reviews first, named approvers sign
off, and then the decision is broadcast to the company. The Rust RFC process
contributes the close date: a final comment period after which the decision
stands.

Put together, that is the five-line header in the reference. Nothing in it
is clever; all of it is routinely skipped.

## 3. Ask precisely (3 min)

		<>

			decision (§3, ADR 0002); gaps in the agent path (§3, two routes to the docs). <strong>Not wanted:</strong>{' '}
			naming, prose, the business model.
			<br />
			<strong>Decider:</strong> me. <strong>Approver:</strong> A. <strong>Input:</strong> B, C.
			<br />
			<strong>Closes:</strong> Fri 24 Oct. Silence is consent. Comment in the doc.
		</>
	}
	note="The second version takes a reviewer two minutes to act on and tells them when they can stop thinking about it."
/>

## 4. Do it (30 min now, 30 later)

Now: write the header on the DocuDex doc; link the ADRs, the POC write-up
and both diagrams from it so a reviewer has one entry point; pick the
reviewers and send it.

Public replies skew to preference and evidence rather than challenge; that is
what the triage table is for. Do not argue in any thread — answer in the
doc, reply with a pointer. Three channels with one close date produce one
review outcome, not three.

On the close date: triage, answer in the doc, write **Review outcome** (what
changed, what was raised and not changed and why, the status line), send one
broadcast message, change the status.

<Checklist
	title="Self-review: the request and the close"
	items={[
		{ question: 'Exactly one decider, and approvers only where someone can actually block?', why: 'Three approvers is a committee; the doc will wait on the slowest.' },
		{ question: 'Does "wanted" name sections and decisions, and does "not wanted" exist?', why: 'The not-wanted line is what stops the week being spent on prose.' },
		{ question: 'Is there a close date, and does it say silence is consent?', why: 'Without it the review never ends and the decision is never recorded.' },
		{ question: 'Can a reviewer reach the ADRs, POC and diagrams from the doc in one click each?', why: 'A reviewer who has to ask for context reviews the context instead of the design.' },
		{ question: 'Did every challenge and gap get answered in the doc, not only in the thread?', why: 'The doc is read in a month; the thread is not.' },
		{ question: 'Does the review outcome list what was raised and NOT changed, with why?', why: 'Both columns. A review outcome that only lists changes hides the decisions you held.' },
		{ question: 'Was one broadcast message sent to the FYI line?', why: 'HashiCorp’s pattern. The review is over when the decision is announced, not when comments stop.' },
	]}
/>
